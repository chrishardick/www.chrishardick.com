401(k) Plans

  • A Retirement Account which can be provided by For Profit Companies for their employees; i.e. it's Company Administered.

  • Contributions are from Pre-tax (gross) salary.

  • Any income earned grows tax free until withdrawn.

  • Employers can match employee contributions. The amount matched by the employer is tax deductible for the employer. However, there may be a number of years before the employer's contribution to your 401(k) plan becomes vested (is actually yours). In other words, if the terms of your company's 401(k) plan state that the employers contributions are not vested for 5 years, and you quit or are fired any time prior to the 5 years, you lose the entire amount that your employer contributed to your 401(k).

  • The Federal Government limits the amount which can be contributed ($10,500 in 2000; up from $10,000 in 1999). However each plan can impose further limits; i.e. a certain percentage of your salary; check to see if your plan imposes these further restrictions.

  • If you retire at 55 or higher, you can withdraw funds without any penalty-any amount; without any minimums.

  • At 59 1/2, regardless if you have retired or not, you can withdraw funds without any penalty.

  • If you have retired and are 70 1/2 years, minimum withdrawal rules kick in.

  • Taking a loan against from your 401(k): 401(k)'s are unique in that they allow you to take a loan from your own funds before your 59 1/2 for the following reasons: (1) hardship, (2) buying a house, (3) financing an education. You must pay the loan back with regular payments. You must pay yourself back both principal and interest. In general, this is not such a great idea, because while your paying yourself interest, it's probably not as much as it would have earned. I'm not sure who defines the amount of interest you must pay; it may be self-imposed with a minimum.

  • Withdrawal Penalties-Normal Tax Rate + 10% Penalty: If you withdraw money from your 401(k) as money other than a loan, you must pay tax on any pre-tax contributions and on the growth, plus, you also pay an additional 10% penalty to the government.

  • Changing Jobs: Since 401(k)'s are company administered and every plan is different, changing jobs can affect your 401(k) plan significantly. Some companies will allow you to keep your savings in the program until you're 59 1/2. Other companies require you to immediately take your money out. If so, your new company might allow you to "rollover" your contributions into your new company's 401(k). If this is not possible you can rollover the funds into an IRA. However 401(k) plans have many advantages over IRA's so rolling over into your new company's 401(K) is usually the better choice. However, prior to 1992, you would receive the rollover money in the form of a check in which you had 60 days to deposit into your new retirement account (either 401(k) or IRA). Post 1992, you should utilize a "direct rollover" in which a check goes directly from your previous company's plan to your new company's plan. If you do not utilize the direct rollover, the withdrawal is immediately subject to withholding tax of 20%, which-like all withholdings-your previous employer is required to ship off to the IRS. The remaining 80% must be rolled over within 60 days to a new retirement account (401(k) or IRA), or else it's subject to the 10% penalty, mentioned above. If you do not roll-over the funds, obviously they will be taxed. The 20% withholding can only be recovered using a special form filed with your next tax return. However, in order to keep the 20% which is now being withheld in the tax-free status, it must be rolled into a new retirement account within 60 days. But where can this come from since you can not regain your withholding until after you file your tax returns-other than your own pocket? In other words, do NOT utilize the Indirect Rollover.

  • An Indirect Rollover Example: Let's suppose you have $10,000 in a 401(k), and you withdraw the money in order to roll it over into your new company's 401(k). You receive a check for $8,000 (remember you had $10,000 in your 401(k), but 20% just went to Uncle Sam for a withholding tax). Now remember, you had $10,000 in tax-exempt status which was earning tax-free interest. You now have 2 choices. (1) You can now put up $2,000 of your own money, and then file the above mentioned form to regain your $2,000 or (2) you can forget about your $2,000 and pay both tax and the 10% penalty on it.
